# Japan country guide — research draft for review

**Audience:** U.S. passport holders planning tourism, business visits, study, work, transit, or a longer stay.  
**Research checked:** October 28, 2026; CDC health section refreshed October 29, 2026.  
**Status:** Draft only. Japan entries in Supabase remain `needs_review`; none of this content is approved or published traveler guidance.

This is a country-level foundation, not a city itinerary or a substitute for a visa decision, clinician, insurer, or emergency authority. Requirements depend on passport, purpose, length of stay, route, medicines, and personal circumstances. Dynamic rules and alerts must be rechecked before launch and again before travel. Local laws and venue rules can differ across prefectures, operators, and facilities.

## 1. Entry, passport, and stay type

- Japan lists the United States among countries whose nationals may enter visa-exempt for qualifying short-term stays. For most listed countries, the landing permission is 90 days. For U.S. citizens visiting for tourism or qualifying business, the U.S. State Department says the passport must remain valid for the full stay, a return or onward ticket should be available, and visitors may not work in Japan's local economy or change to another status while there.
- For a planned stay over 90 days, Japan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs says travelers should follow a work or long-stay visa route; in principle, they should obtain a Certificate of Eligibility through the Immigration Services Agency before applying for a visa at the Japanese mission responsible for where they live. The right route depends on the reason for travel, and a Certificate of Eligibility does not guarantee that a visa will be issued. Paid work also needs the correct status even when a stay is shorter than 90 days.
- Treat visa-free entry as a limited short-stay pathway, not a general permission for any trip. Study, residency, extended stays, and some business activities require checking the Japanese status/visa rules before booking. Transit depends on itinerary, airport, whether the traveler passes immigration, and onward-country documents; confirm with the airline and Japanese authorities.
- Keep passport, itinerary, accommodation/contact details, and any required onward travel evidence accessible. Confirm the passport is in good condition and check airline document rules. Dual nationals should check which passport they must use for each border.
- Sources: [Japan Ministry of Foreign Affairs visa exemption list](https://www.mofa.go.jp/j_info/visit/visa/short/novisa.html), [Japan MOFA visa information](https://www.mofa.go.jp/j_info/visit/visa/index.html) (page updated June 24, 2026), [U.S. State Department Japan information](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/japan.html). Verify category-specific advice for the exact purpose and duration.

## 2. Getting there and border arrival

- Japan Customs describes arrival as a sequence that can involve quarantine, immigration, applicable animal/plant quarantine, and customs. Follow signs and instructions at the actual port of entry; airport layouts and steps differ.
- Visit Japan Web offers electronic immigration and customs declaration procedures. Check the live official portal and its manual shortly before departure to confirm what can be completed online, whether it applies to the traveler, and what must still be presented in person. A QR code or online form does not replace passport, visa/status, or other required entry documents.
- Declare goods and follow customs and quarantine rules. Food, plants, animal products, medicines, and high-value items can have special restrictions. Check official rules before packing rather than assuming an item is allowed because it is for personal use.
- Sources: [Japan Customs passenger clearance](https://www.customs.go.jp/english/summary/passenger.htm), [Visit Japan Web English manual](https://www.vjw.digital.go.jp/manual/main/visitjapanweb_manual_en.pdf), [Japan MHLW medicine import guidance](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/seisakunitsuite/bunya/kenkou_iryou/iyakuhin/kojinyunyu/topics/tp010401-1_00001.html).

## 3. Getting around the country

- Japan has extensive train, subway, and bus networks; service, payment methods, accessibility, operating hours, and English support vary by region and operator. Use the relevant operator's current route, fare, and disruption information. Allow extra time for transfers and the first/last mile, especially outside major corridors.
- The Japan Rail Pass is valid only on defined participating Japan Railways (JR) services and subject to its current eligibility, purchase, reservation, and train-specific rules. It does not automatically cover every train, subway, bus, or private railway. Compare the full itinerary and current fare/pass rules before buying.
- Taxis can help with luggage, late arrivals, or places without convenient transit; payment acceptance varies. Confirm destination in writing or on a map. If renting/driving, check current U.S. license and permit requirements with Japanese authorities and rental company before departure; do not assume a U.S. license alone is sufficient.
- Sources: [U.S. State Department Japan information](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/japan.html), [Japan Rail Pass coverage and conditions](https://japanrailpass.net/en/about_jrp/), [Japan National Police Agency traffic rules](https://www.npa.go.jp/english/bureau/traffic/document/TrafficSafetyRules.pdf). This guide does not provide route-specific schedules or fares.

## 4. Currency, payments, and customs cash declaration

- Japan's currency is the yen (¥ / JPY). Bank of Japan pages explain current and valid banknotes and coins. Carry a backup payment method and some yen for places that do not accept cards; card and ATM acceptance still depends on the merchant, card network, bank, hours, and location.
- Ask the card issuer about international use, foreign transaction fees, travel notices, ATM fees, and fraud controls. When offered a choice at a card terminal or ATM, check the displayed currency and conversion terms before confirming. Exchange rates change; RoamReady should not publish a static USD/JPY rate without a timestamped live source.
- Japan Customs requires a declaration when bringing into or taking out of Japan more than ¥1,000,000 or equivalent in covered cash and monetary instruments. This is a declaration threshold, not a maximum amount; check the Customs page for which instruments count and current procedures.
- Sources: [U.S. State Department Japan information](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/japan.html), [Bank of Japan notes and coins](https://www.boj.or.jp/en/note_tfjgs/note/valid/), [Japan Customs passenger clearance](https://www.customs.go.jp/english/summary/passenger.htm).

## 5. Safety, weather, and disruptions

- At the research check date, the U.S. State Department listed Japan at Level 1, “Exercise normal precautions”; the advisory level itself was dated May 15, 2025. Recheck the current advisory, local notices, and airline updates before travel because risk ratings and conditions can change.
- Official U.S. guidance identifies earthquakes, typhoons, tsunamis, landslides, and flooding among hazards to plan for. Travelers should know their accommodation's evacuation instructions, monitor local alerts and weather, and follow Japanese authorities' instructions. Conditions can interrupt rail, flights, roads, and access to services.
- Use normal precautions with belongings and when going out. Laws around drugs, medicines, identification, driving, and other conduct can differ substantially from U.S. rules and may carry serious consequences. Read current country guidance; avoid turning a general country overview into assumptions about every neighborhood or venue.
- Sources: [U.S. State Department Japan advisory](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/japan.html), [Japan Meteorological Agency](https://www.jma.go.jp/jma/indexe.html), [Japan Tourism Agency responsible-traveler resources](https://www.mlit.go.jp/kankocho/responsible-traveler/en.html).

## 6. Health, care, and medicine

- CDC's Japan Traveler View was last reviewed August 25, 2026; we rechecked it October 29, 2026. It asks travelers to check vaccines and medicines and see a health professional at least a month before departure. It advises travelers to be up to date on routine vaccines and COVID-19 vaccines for which they are eligible. It says to consider hepatitis A vaccine for most travelers (and recommends it for some higher-risk activities such as street-food eating or rural travel); recommends hepatitis B vaccine for unvaccinated travelers younger than 60, while unvaccinated travelers 60 and older may get vaccinated; and recommends Japanese encephalitis vaccine for people moving to or spending long periods in affected areas or frequent travelers. For shorter trips, JE vaccine is a consideration when activities increase exposure (for example rural outdoor activities or lodging without screens/air conditioning), and is not recommended for short-term urban-only travel. These are CDC recommendations to discuss with a clinician, not individual medical advice.
- The Japan page currently displays a Level 1 “Practice Usual Precautions” Travel Health Notice that links to Global Measles, dated May 28, 2025. That is a global notice, not a Japan-specific measles outbreak alert. CDC says all international travelers should be fully vaccinated against measles with MMR under its international travel recommendations. Check the live notice list and its date again before publication or travel.
- The same CDC page lists dengue, leptospirosis, hantavirus, tuberculosis, and tick-borne encephalitis among its non-vaccine-preventable disease topics. CDC recommends practical precautions such as avoiding bug bites and contaminated floodwater, using safe food and water practices, preparing for outdoor conditions and heat, avoiding contact with unfamiliar animals, and seeking medical care after animal bites or scratches. Its health-care planning advice includes checking insurance, considering travel-health and medical-evacuation cover, carrying a local-language card for conditions/allergies/medicine names, and carrying prescription copies.
- Do not assume a U.S. prescription makes a medicine legal to import. Japan's rules depend on ingredient, classification, quantity, and duration. Some medicines require prior Import Confirmation or other authorization; some are prohibited or controlled. Check each active ingredient with Japan's MHLW before packing and before departure. Do not rely on a brand name alone.
- U.S. health insurance may not cover care abroad. State Department guidance says travelers should check coverage; medical facilities may require payment up front. Consider appropriate medical and evacuation coverage for the trip and keep insurer assistance details accessible.
- Sources: [CDC Japan Traveler View](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/children.chronic/japan), [CDC Travel Health Notices](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/notices), [Japan MHLW personal medicine import rules](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/seisakunitsuite/bunya/kenkou_iryou/iyakuhin/kojinyunyu/topics/tp010401-1_00001.html), [U.S. State Department Japan information](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/japan.html).

## 7. Local culture and respectful travel

- Japan is not culturally uniform; customs, expectations, and facility rules vary. Observe signs and local instructions, ask before photographing people or private spaces, keep shared areas clean, and be considerate of noise and other travelers. The Japan Tourism Agency specifically promotes considerate photography, cleaning up, and avoiding large luggage in crowded places.
- Remove shoes where requested and follow posted directions at homes, some accommodations, religious sites, and other facilities. On public transport, keep calls and noise low, queue where marked, and keep bags from blocking doors or seats. These are practical etiquette cues, not a claim that every place has an identical rule.
- Onsen, bathing, religious, and other facilities may have specific access rules, including rules about tattoos or swimwear; check the facility before arrival rather than assuming a countrywide policy. Ask permission before entering restricted/private areas or photographing ceremonies and worshippers.
- Sources: [Japan Tourism Agency responsible-traveler guidance](https://www.mlit.go.jp/kankocho/responsible-traveler/en.html), [U.S. State Department Japan information](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/japan.html). These recommendations are phrased as respectful practices; facility signs and laws take precedence.

## 8. Phones, internet, and staying connected

- Before leaving, ask the U.S. carrier whether the phone is unlocked and compatible, whether Japan is included in the plan, what roaming/data/call charges apply, and how to contact support. Coverage and service quality vary by provider and location.
- Compare carrier roaming, a travel eSIM, or a local SIM. Confirm device compatibility, activation requirements, data limits, voice/SMS availability, and whether the option works before relying on it. Save offline copies of passport details, accommodation and itinerary, maps, and key contacts; keep a way to receive important alerts.
- Sources: [U.S. State Department communication abroad guidance](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/planning/guidance/communication-abroad.html), [U.S. State Department Japan information](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/japan.html). Provider-specific prices and coverage need current provider sources.

## 9. Electricity, plugs, and device preparation

- Japan's household supply is generally 100 V AC. Frequency is 50 Hz in eastern Japan and 60 Hz in western Japan. Type A two-flat-pin outlets are common; outlet fit can vary by accommodation and location.
- Check each device's input label and the manufacturer's instructions for voltage and frequency. A plug adapter changes physical fit; it does not convert voltage. If a device is not rated for Japan's supply, use a suitable converter only if the manufacturer allows it, or leave the device at home. Take chargers, battery packs, and any needed adapters; airline rules apply to batteries.
- Sources: [Kansai Electric Power electricity guide](https://www.kepco.co.jp/english/home/denki/01.html), [Kagawa University voltage and frequency information](https://www.kagawa-u.ac.jp/kagawa-u_ead/english/newcomer/kagawa-university-international-house/voltage-and-frequency/). The actual socket at a property may differ.

## 10. Emergency help and U.S. consular support

- Japan's emergency numbers are 110 for police and 119 for fire or ambulance. The Fire and Disaster Management Agency's English guide describes calling 119 and giving the emergency type and location; prepare a nearby address or landmark. In a traffic accident, the National Police Agency says to contact police even when no one appears injured.
- At the research check date, the U.S. State Department listed +81-3-3224-5000 as the U.S. Embassy Tokyo main and after-hours number. Confirm the live State Department page for the correct embassy/consulate and current contact method for where you are. Emergency responders handle immediate local emergencies; U.S. consular staff can provide limited assistance to U.S. citizens but do not replace local emergency services, hospitals, insurers, or legal counsel.
- Save emergency numbers, embassy/consulate contacts, insurer assistance, and accommodation information offline. If an emergency is active, follow local responders' instructions first.
- Sources: [U.S. State Department Japan information and contacts](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/japan.html), [Japan National Police Agency traffic rules](https://www.npa.go.jp/english/bureau/traffic/document/TrafficSafetyRules.pdf), [Japan Fire and Disaster Management Agency ambulance guide](https://www.fdma.go.jp/publication/portal/items/portal001_pamphiet_english.pdf).
